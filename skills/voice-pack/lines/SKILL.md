# Lines: what a character says

A line is right when this person, in this moment, would say it. You get there by reasoning from the material first and checking what you wrote after. This skill is only about which words they are; how the words are marked up, timed and spoken belongs to whatever will speak them.

## Work it out from first principles

Start from facts about the person and the moment, not from how lines of this kind usually go. Answer these from the material before you write a word:

1. **This person.** Their age, what fills their day, what they care about and what they fear, who matters to them. How they talk: long sentences or short ones, plain words or careful ones, what they call themselves and other people, the words they keep reaching for.
2. **This scene.** Where they are, what their hands are doing, what has just happened, and who is in front of them: what that person is to them and what each knows of the other. Your task may give part of the scene (who is listening, what the words are for); the material gives the rest.
3. **What they want right now.** What is on their mind at this moment, what they want from the person in front of them, and what they would rather keep to themselves.
4. **What comes out of their mouth.** Out loud, now, to this listener: what would they actually say? People answer what is in front of them, in their own words, and leave out what both sides already know.

A single line, or a short passage, is one moment, not a portrait. It lands on what is in front of them right now: the thing in their hands, what the listener just did, what is about to happen. Who they are comes through in how they say it, not in a summary of themselves: nobody tells someone who already knows them their own name, their family or their rules. They introduce themselves only when the material makes this moment a first meeting.

A ship's cook, proud of his soup, who has fed this crew for twenty years.
- A portrait: "I'm the cook on this ship, twenty years now, and my soup is the best on the sea."
- A moment: "Bowl. No, the big one. And blow on it first, it's still bubbling."

Then write the words. A line you could have written without reading the material is not done: go back to step 1.[^b1]

## Check it

Read what you wrote against each check, rewrite what fails, and read it again. You are done when everything you wrote passes all four.[^b2]

### 1. Keep the secret

A truth the material keeps from the listener stays unsaid. It may show a little, in how something is said or in what the person does, and no more: the secret is what the listener comes back for.

A lighthouse keeper whose daughter drowned last winter; he tells visitors she moved to the city.
- Says it: "Since my girl drowned out there, I keep the lamp lit every night for her."
- Keeps it: "That's her coat on the hook. Leave it. She'll be wanting it."

### 2. Concrete, not stock

A line that would fit in anyone's mouth says nothing about this person. Replace it with what only they would say: their trade, their habit, the thing in their hands, the way they notice things.

An old cobbler at a market stall, short with words, proud that he can hear a worn heel.
- Stock: 「欢迎回来！今天有什么需要，随时跟我说哦。」 ("Welcome back! Whatever you need today, just tell me.")
- Concrete: 「又是这双？搁这儿。左脚后跟，你一进门我就听出来了。」 ("These again? Put them here. Left heel: I heard it the moment you came in.")

### 3. Invent almost nothing

Every fact in the line can be found in the material: names, places, past events, how people stand with each other. You may add one small thing the person has at hand or does, when it fits them. A new event, a new person or a new past is not yours to add.

A night-shift nurse; the material says she has worked the ward for ten years and hates the coffee from the machine.
- Invents: "Ever since my brother died on this ward, I've never left a patient alone at night."
- From the material: "Ten years of nights and this machine still gives me mud. Here, you take it. I'll get another."

### 4. Keep the register

Say it as rough, as cold, as formal or as playful as the material has the person: no softer and no harder. Polish a crude person into politeness and they are gone; push a gruff one into cruelty, or a warm one into gushing, and they are gone too.

A retired sergeant who runs a boxing gym: he swears in every sentence and looks after the kids who train there. One of them walks in late.
- Sanded down: 「你来啦，先坐下歇一会儿吧，今天我们慢慢练。」 ("You're here, sit down and rest a bit first, we'll take it slow today.")
- Pushed past: 「滚出去！再迟到一次，腿给你打断！」 ("Get out! Be late once more and I'll break your legs!")
- Kept: 「他娘的又迟到？绷带缠上，先跳二百下绳。」 ("Late again, damn it? Wrap your hands and skip two hundred first.")
